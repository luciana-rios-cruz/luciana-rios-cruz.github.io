Tractor Cab Climate Control — John Deere

Academic Team Project | Tecnológico de Monterrey | October 2025

Analog temperature control, thermal modeling, and a functional scale tractor prototype.

PROJECT OVERVIEW

Our team developed a scale prototype to demonstrate how heat from a simulated tractor engine compartment could warm an operator’s cab. The project integrated an analog control circuit, forced-air ventilation, thermal calculations, and a 3D-printed tractor structure.

An electric air heater represented engine heat, while a fan transferred warm air into the cab.

THE ENGINEERING CHALLENGE

The objective was to regulate cab temperature within a 34–36°C target range, using operational amplifiers rather than a microcontroller-based controller.

The main challenges were amplifying the temperature sensor’s small voltage signal, implementing stable ON/OFF switching, and integrating the thermal and electronic components into a compact prototype.

SYSTEM OPERATING CONCEPT

Generate heat: an electric heater warms the simulated engine compartment.

Measure temperature: an LM35 sensor monitors the cab.

Condition the signal: operational amplifiers adjust the sensor voltage for the control circuit.

Control airflow: a comparator with hysteresis switches the fan according to temperature.

Transfer heat: forced airflow carries warm air into the cab.

The temperature-control decisions were analog. An Arduino was used separately to send temperature readings to a computer.

ANALOG CIRCUIT DESIGN

The circuit used three 741 operational amplifiers to condition the LM35 temperature signal and generate an ON/OFF command for the fan.

HYSTERESIS & SWITCHING

Positive feedback created two switching thresholds, allowing the comparator to retain its previous state between them and reduce rapid fan switching.

The documented design used 10 kΩ and 50 kΩ resistors, with a 3.6 V reference. The intended heating sequence was to activate ventilation at 34°C and deactivate it at 36°C. Actual switching temperatures require verification, particularly after correcting the signal-conditioning offset.

ELECTRONIC SIMULATION & ASSEMBLY

The analog circuit was modeled in LTspice before being assembled on a breadboard.

Physical testing required several iterations to address resistor-value differences, disconnected breadboard sections, operational-amplifier saturation, and sensor supply problems. These adjustments helped connect the simulated design with the behavior of real components.

CAD DESIGN & PROTOTYPE FABRICATION

The tractor was modeled in SolidWorks and 3D-printed in PLA, with transparent acrylic cab walls for visibility.

The structure integrated three main compartments:

Simulated engine: 14.5 × 9 × 7.5 cm, housing the heater.

Operator’s cab: 13 × 12 × 9 cm, containing the temperature sensor.

Electronics compartment: 7 × 12 × 9 cm, accommodating the circuit and monitoring hardware.

A fan installed between the engine compartment and cab provided forced ventilation.

HEAT TRANSFER ANALYSIS

The team combined CAD-derived air volumes with air properties to develop a simplified thermal model.

Key documented inputs included:

Ambient temperature: 25°C.

Engine-compartment modeling temperature: 40°C.

Cab modeling temperature: 35°C.

Heat-transfer area: 0.0063 m².

Engine-compartment air volume: approximately 0.682 L.

Cab air volume: approximately 1.114 L.

These values supported estimates of air mass and temperature change over time.

THERMAL RESPONSE GRAPHS

Simulated Engine Compartment

The Excel model predicts a temperature increase from 25°C toward 40°C, with the heating rate decreasing as the compartment approaches its modeled equilibrium.

Operator’s Cab

The cab model predicts a slower increase from 25°C toward 35°C, illustrating the thermal response associated with heating the enclosed air.

Both graphs are calculated model predictions, rather than measured temperature histories or direct evidence of closed-loop regulation.

PROTOTYPE TESTING & RESULTS

The team built and tested an integrated prototype combining the heater, fan, sensor, analog circuit, and printed enclosure.

The report describes successful operation within the intended thermal range after troubleshooting. However, it does not provide a measured temperature-versus-time dataset establishing regulation accuracy, overshoot, or repeatability.

The prototype demonstrated the integration of analog electronics and forced-air heating at scale.

TECHNICAL CHALLENGES & NEXT STEPS

Resistor tolerances, breadboard discontinuities, amplifier saturation, and incorrect sensor supply conditions affected circuit operation.

Further development would prioritize a PCB, improved electrical connections, verified switching thresholds, heater protection, and recorded temperature tests. These measurements would allow a more rigorous comparison between the thermal model and prototype performance.

MY CONTRIBUTION

I contributed to resistor calculations, analog-circuit implementation, and troubleshooting during prototype development.

My work included investigating differences between calculated and measured resistance values, identifying breadboard continuity problems, and examining how feedback connections affected amplifier saturation and hysteresis behavior.

WHAT I LEARNED

I strengthened my understanding of signal conditioning, hysteresis, and the practical limitations of analog circuits.

The project taught me to verify component values, supply conditions, and electrical continuity systematically, while connecting circuit behavior with the thermal response of a physical system.
