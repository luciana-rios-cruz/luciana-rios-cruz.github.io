Hydraulic Cutting Attachment — John Deere

Academic Team Project | Tecnológico de Monterrey | May 2025

Mechanical design, material selection, and manufacturing planning for a hydraulic tree-cutting attachment intended for a John Deere 512 front loader.

PROJECT OVERVIEW

Our team developed a hydraulic cutting attachment concept for cutting and handling logs. The proposal combined a scissor-type cutting mechanism, upper and lower gripping elements, and a supporting frame.

The project included CAD modeling, design failure analysis, material comparisons, manufacturing process planning, and a preliminary component cost estimate.

THE ENGINEERING CHALLENGE

Design an attachment that could hold a log during cutting and subsequent handling while considering loader compatibility, structural support, hydraulic actuation, and manufacturability.

MECHANISM MOTION & EXPLODED VIEW — CAD ANIMATIONS

The first video demonstrates the opening and closing motion of the attachment’s gripping arms, showing how they rotate around their pivot joints to grip and release a log.

The second video presents an exploded view of the CAD assembly, separating the components to illustrate their arrangement, connections, and relationship within the complete mechanism.

MAIN COMPONENTS & THEIR FUNCTIONS

Upper gripping jaws: stabilize the log and limit unwanted movement during cutting.

Upper support & pivot assembly: supports the upper jaws and provides their pivot connections.

Structural support frame: connects the upper and lower assemblies and supports the mechanism.

Loader mounting bracket: provides the proposed connection between the attachment and the loader.

Lower gripping jaw: supports and retains the log during cutting and handling.

Gripper hydraulic cylinder: actuates the lower gripping jaw to hold or release the log.

Cutting blade & hydraulic actuator: perform the cutting motion by transferring hydraulic force to the blade.

HYDRAULIC OPERATING CONCEPT

The concept used hydraulic cylinders to actuate the cutting and gripping elements.

The intended operating sequence was:

Position: approach and align the attachment with the log.

Grip: close the jaws to stabilize the material.

Cut: actuate the blade while maintaining the grip.

Retain: hold the separated log for handling.

Release: open the jaws at the intended unloading location.

Cylinder sizing, hydraulic pressure requirements, and the resulting cutting force would require further engineering validation.

DESIGN FAILURE ANALYSIS — DFMEA

The team evaluated potential failures involving the cutting arm, hydraulic cylinders, blade, jaws, structural profiles, and upper support.

The analysis considered failure causes, effects, severity, occurrence, detection, and recommended actions.

BLADE MATERIAL SELECTION

The team compared AISI 9260 low-alloy steel and AISI 1080 carbon steel, both in an oil-quenched and tempered condition at 425°C.

Three material-selection charts supported the comparison:

Price per volume vs. yield strength: AISI 9260 offered higher resistance to permanent deformation in the selected dataset.

Density vs. Young’s modulus: the materials had similar density and stiffness, so these properties provided limited differentiation.

Density vs. specific strength: AISI 9260 offered greater strength relative to its weight.

AISI 9260 was proposed for the blade based primarily on its strength advantage. These charts supported material screening rather than demonstrating cutting performance or service life.

STRUCTURAL MATERIAL SELECTION

The supporting structure was evaluated using S275, S355, and ASTM A36 structural steels.

The comparison examined:

Yield strength and price: resistance to permanent deformation relative to material cost.

Fatigue strength at 10⁷ cycles: comparative performance under repeated loading.

Young’s modulus: elastic stiffness.

Density: material mass for a given component volume.

S355 was proposed because the selected dataset showed a favorable strength and fatigue comparison, while density and stiffness were broadly similar across the candidates.

MANUFACTURING PROCESS PLANNING

The manufacturing proposal separated upstream material production from attachment fabrication. Purchased steel stock served as the starting point for the proposed production activities.

Component-specific process diagrams covered:

Plates: stock preparation, cutting and machining, joining, finishing, and inspection.

Cutting blade: AISI 9260 stock preparation, laser cutting, heat treatment, edge grinding, finishing, and inspection.

Square tubes: cutting and forming, preliminary assembly, welding, finishing, and inspection.

Support tubes: cutting and forming, joining, finishing, and inspection.

These diagrams outlined proposed routes. Detailed welding procedures, heat-treatment sequencing, tolerances, and inspection criteria would need refinement before manufacturing.

PRELIMINARY COST ANALYSIS

The team used a Swift-based model to compare component manufacturing costs:

Mi = V × Cmt + Rc × Pc

V: material volume per component.

Cmt: material cost per unit volume.

Rc: relative processing factor.

Pc: basic processing cost.

The spreadsheet combined CAD-derived volumes, component quantities, material costs, and processing assumptions for a planned output of 1,000 units per year.

The report calculated approximately USD 163.5 per attachment under its simplified assumptions. Machine operating costs were set to zero on the assumption that existing equipment would be used. This figure should therefore be treated as a preliminary model result requiring revision before use as a production estimate.

PROJECT OUTCOMES & NEXT STEPS

The project produced a CAD assembly, a design risk assessment, proposed blade and structural materials, manufacturing process diagrams, and a preliminary cost model.

Further development would require structural and hydraulic calculations, mounting verification, prototype testing, and a more complete manufacturing cost assessment.
