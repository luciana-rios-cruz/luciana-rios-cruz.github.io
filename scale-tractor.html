Autonomous Scale Tractor — John Deere

Academic Team Project | Tecnológico de Monterrey | December 2025

Embedded control, sensor integration, and autonomous navigation development for a scale agricultural vehicle.

PROJECT OVERVIEW

Our team developed a scale tractor prototype using an STM32F103RB microcontroller. The system combined encoder-based speed control, inertial heading correction, ultrasonic obstacle detection, and Bluetooth communication for manual commands and telemetry.

CAD-MODEL

The CAD model shows the scale vehicle’s two-level chassis and component layout. The upper platform houses the STM32 controller and front-facing ultrasonic sensor, while the lower section accommodates the drivetrain. Isometric and front views illustrate the mechanical assembly and available mounting space.

THE ENGINEERING CHALLENGE

Integrate sensing, control, and communication into a physical vehicle capable of following a square route with 5-meter sides. The challenge required coordinated speed regulation, distance estimation, steering, and obstacle response.

HARDWARE & SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

STM32F103RB: embedded processing and peripheral coordination.

DC motor and incremental encoder: propulsion, speed measurement, and distance estimation.

TB6612FNG driver: motor direction and PWM control.

MG996R servo: steering actuation.

MPU6050 IMU: inertial measurements and heading estimation.

HC-SR04: obstacle distance measurement.

HC-05 Bluetooth: wireless commands and telemetry.

SPEED CONTROL & MOTION PROFILE

A PID controller regulated vehicle speed using encoder feedback, with updates every 50 ms (20 Hz). A trapezoidal reference profile divided movement into acceleration, constant-speed travel, and deceleration.

The documented implementation used Kp = 8.0, Ki = 2.0, and Kd = 0.4, with the motor command limited to a normalized 0–1 PWM output.

The position plot compares the commanded distance (orange) with encoder-based displacement (blue). The reference reaches approximately 4 m at 18 s, while the vehicle reaches it around 25–26 s, showing a tracking delay during this test.

MICROCONTROLLER CONFIGURATION

The STM32F103RB was configured in STM32CubeIDE to coordinate motor control, steering, sensor acquisition, and serial communication through dedicated peripherals.

GPIO & external interrupts: digital outputs controlled motor direction and ultrasonic triggering. Encoder interrupts captured wheel movement, while the onboard push button reset motion and controller variables.

I²C2 — 100 kHz: connected the MPU6050 for acceleration and angular-rate measurements, using 7-bit addressing.

USART interfaces: asynchronous serial communication supported commands, telemetry, and debugging. The configuration documented USART1 at 9,600 bps and USART2 at 115,200 bps, both with 8-bit data.

TIM1 — motor PWM: generated the duty-cycle signal used by the motor driver to regulate propulsion.

TIM2 — 50 ms control period: triggered speed estimation, PID updates, and trapezoidal motion-profile calculations at 20 Hz.

TIM3 — ultrasonic timing: provided a 1 µs timer resolution for measuring the echo duration and calculating obstacle distance.

TIM4 — 50 Hz servo PWM: generated steering pulses with a 20 ms period.

SWD interface: supported microcontroller programming and debugging through ST-Link.

HEADING ESTIMATION & STEERING

The MPU6050 provided inertial measurements for orientation estimation. Calibration compensated sensor bias, while Kalman filtering supported roll and pitch estimation; yaw was obtained through gyroscope integration.

A proportional heading controller adjusted the steering servo relative to its 90° neutral position, with a minimum update interval of 20 ms.

OBSTACLE DETECTION & WIRELESS CONTROL

Ultrasonic sensing supported obstacle-triggered stopping. Bluetooth enabled manual forward, reverse, stop, and steering commands, along with telemetry such as yaw angle.

STM32CubeMonitor was used to visualize speed and position variables during testing.

AUTONOMOUS NAVIGATION

The square-route routine used a state machine to coordinate:

Initialization: reset segment distance and establish the heading reference.

Straight travel: regulate speed and correct heading.

Segment completion: stop after reaching the commanded distance.

Turning: execute the steering maneuver and update orientation.

Repetition: advance through the remaining sides.

TESTING & RESULTS

Speed regulation: the team reported smooth speed tracking during acceleration and deceleration tests.

Heading correction: steering feedback supported straight-line travel, with some remaining offset.

Obstacle response: the vehicle stopped when objects entered the configured detection range.

Communication: Bluetooth commands and telemetry were functional, although connection instability occurred during development.

Square navigation: the routine produced an irregular square and did not consistently complete all four sides accurately.

TECHNICAL CHALLENGES & IMPROVEMENTS

Distance-sign errors, controller tuning, motion-profile timing, and wiring reliability affected repeatability. Further development would prioritize encoder direction verification, more consistent electrical connections, and separate validation of straight travel and turning before repeating the complete route. Additional testing would check sensor calibration, power stability, and telemetry to identify positioning errors and improve steering consistency across trials.

MY CONTRIBUTION

I contributed to embedded-system development and integration, working with encoder-based odometry, ultrasonic sensing, inertial estimation, and PID speed regulation. I also gained hands-on experience programming and debugging the STM32, troubleshooting connections, and monitoring system behavior. I also created the complete CAD model of the scale tractor.

WHAT I LEARNED

I strengthened my ability to connect control theory with physical hardware. The project showed me how sensor calibration, controller tuning, timing, power distribution, and wiring quality jointly determine the reliability of an embedded system.
